The question

Can a model tell when its steering is wrong?

A camera image goes in and a steering angle comes out. That’s the basic idea behind behavioral cloning: learn from someone’s driving demonstrations. I wanted to look past the average error and see what happened when the view changed.

I built RobustDrive around two questions: which visual changes make steering worse, and can a model’s uncertainty help identify those bad predictions?

What I built

From a recorded drive to a steering model.

I recorded a drive in the Udacity simulator and trained a compact PyTorch convolutional network from scratch. The first usable recording contains 9,459 rows of camera and steering data. I compared a center-camera baseline, a version with recovery cameras and visual augmentation, and a version that also used dropout.

I split the recording in time order, leaving gaps between the training, validation, and test sets so neighboring frames wouldn’t land directly across a boundary. The held-out test set has 1,404 center-camera frames.

Around the model, I built data checks, YAML experiment configs, checkpointing, training resume, automatic evaluation, pytest tests, and GitHub Actions CI. The same preprocessing is used for training, evaluation, and simulator driving.

The tests

Make the view harder, one change at a time.

The benchmark applies 11 visual changes at five nonzero severity levels: darker or brighter images, reduced contrast, Gaussian and motion blur, noise, occlusion, horizontal and vertical shifts, shadows, and color shifts. That makes 55 conditions on the same held-out frames.

For uncertainty, I ran the dropout model 20 times on each image and measured how much its steering predictions varied. I then checked whether that variation identified large errors, and whether keeping only the least uncertain predictions reduced the remaining error.

What I found

The simplest trained model did best.

The added cameras and augmentation didn’t beat the baseline on this recording. The uncertainty experiment was also a negative result: its high-error detection ROC-AUC was 0.2837. Keeping the least uncertain half of predictions made mean absolute error worse, from 0.09783 to 0.13040.

Live camera-to-steering control worked in the simulator. I haven’t established a lap-completion rate or advance warning before a road departure; those need complete event annotations and separate driving trials.

What I’m taking from it

A low average error can miss the hard turns.

Most of the recorded steering labels were close to straight ahead. An always-straight reference even scored a lower MAE than the learned baseline, although its RMSE was worse. That made it clear why one average score can’t stand in for useful driving.

The uncertainty score could also be low on a badly wrong prediction. I kept that result rather than changing the threshold after seeing the test labels. It gives me a more specific problem to investigate.

What’s next

Collect a better second recording.

This first study uses one recording, one track, and one seed. The frames are related, and the augmented runs weren’t matched for compute. I want to collect an independent session with more right turns and recovery examples, isolate the augmentation choices, and repeat the experiments with more seeds.

For live evaluation, the next step is to record complete lap, intervention, and failure events at a matched speed. That would let me connect the offline measurements to what the car actually does in the simulator.
