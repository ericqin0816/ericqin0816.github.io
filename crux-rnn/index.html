CruxRNN.

A climbing question became a learning experiment.

What changes when you repeat one pattern or mix several together? I used a small recurrent neural network to study how the answer depends on how similar those patterns are.

Where it started

The same moves, in a different order.

Speed climbing involves a lot of repetition. That made me curious about practice itself: doing one pattern over and over, or switching between several. I found a 2026 paper in Scientific Reports by Song, Kim, and Kim that explored practice schedules with a minimal recurrent neural network, and started by reproducing its behavior.

Then I added a question of my own. If the patterns are nearly identical, does mixing them help as much as it does when the patterns are very different? Climbing gave me the motivation; the experiments use artificial sequences in a computer.

The experiment

Change the similarity. Keep the comparison fair.

I used a small Elman RNN with seven input units, seven hidden units, and four possible actions at each sequence position. Three seven-action sequences differed at exactly 1, 2, 4, or 7 positions. That count is their Hamming distance: a larger number means the patterns are less alike.

For each distance, I ran 30 paired comparisons. Both networks in a pair started from the same weights and saw the same 15,000 training examples over 750 optimizer updates. One learned the patterns in blocks; the other saw them mixed together. That made 120 pairs and 240 trained networks in the final experiment.

I used separate held-out examples to evaluate them, balanced the six possible block orders, and used 10,000 bootstrap resamples for confidence intervals. The source reproduction, pilot, earlier exploratory run, and fresh final experiment are kept separate in the repository.

What I found

The gap grew as the patterns became less alike.

On fresh noisy examples, interleaved practice had higher average action accuracy at all four distances. Its mean advantage went from 2.1 percentage points when just one position differed to 38.9 points when all seven differed.

The fitted trend was +6.12 percentage points per additional differing position, with a 95% bootstrap interval of 5.38–6.85. At distance 1, the interval for the mean gap crossed zero, so the small apparent advantage there is uncertain.

What changed my view

The score you choose matters.

Action accuracy only asks how many individual positions are right. It can look good while the model still gets the full pattern wrong. Interleaving had lower average complete-sequence accuracy at distances 2, 4, and 7, and performance on unseen sequence bases stayed close to the four-action chance level.

That changes how I describe the result. The experiment shows a pattern in one measure of learning under this setup. It doesn’t support a broad claim that interleaving makes every kind of learning better.

What it can tell us

A small model leaves some big questions open.

The initial cue contains the whole target sequence, so the network is learning to decode a numerical cue. There are no human participants, brain recordings, or measured climbing improvements. I also kept the architecture and training budget fixed, and the action frequencies weren’t matched across all conditions.

The next questions are about those choices: whether the pattern holds with different cue designs, better control of action frequencies, and other model sizes. The repository includes the experiment history, code, saved results, and a walkthrough so the finding can be checked.
