Where it started

I wanted more than the finish time.

I compete in speed climbing, where a run is over in seconds. The finish time is useful, but it doesn’t show where one attempt differed from another. I wanted a better way to watch my footage back and compare what happened along the wall.

ClimbIQ grew out of that: load a race recording, find the useful moments, and go back to the frame when something needs a closer look.

Using it

One place to watch, check, and compare.

The app runs in the browser and keeps the video on the device. It suggests race events and split times, lets me step through individual frames, and gives me a way to adjust a marker when it doesn’t look right.

Attempts can be saved locally, compared, and exported. I wanted the analysis and the footage beside each other, so checking a result stays part of the normal workflow.

How it works

Three signals from the same race.

The analysis combines changes in the timing lights, audio cues, and the climber’s motion. They give different clues about when a race event happened. A recording with no useful audio can still be reviewed through its frames.

I built the interface in React and TypeScript with Vite. Canvas handles frame and pixel inspection, Web Audio supports audio analysis, and MediaPipe Pose estimates motion using its existing pose model. WebAssembly is part of the browser processing stack.

The signals produce suggested markers. When they disagree, I can inspect the evidence and correct the timing myself.

Testing it

Race footage gets messy.

A video shared through a messaging app can look quite different from the original. I tested compression, resizing, exposure changes, different frame rates, missing audio, and trimming to see how the analysis handled those changes.

I also checked the frame-review behavior itself. If I’m using a frame to correct a marker, I need to know that seeking and inspecting its pixels works as expected.

Where it is now

The review workflow is built.

ClimbIQ brings the video, event suggestions, frame inspection, corrections, attempt comparison, and exports together. It gives me a way to look beyond the final time and check the moments that led up to it.

The testing covers the recordings and variants above. I haven’t established a general timing-accuracy figure, so a suggested split still deserves a look at the footage. Saved attempts stay on the device unless they’re exported.

What I’d like to improve

Try it on more kinds of footage.

Next I’d like to expand the recordings, measure timing error against independently checked events, and make it easier to understand why a marker was suggested. Feedback from other climbers would also help me decide which comparisons are most useful in training.
